 4. Competitive Landscape

The quantitative finance space is dominated by traditional models, but alternatives are emerging due to Black-Scholes' shortcomings (e.g., assumes constant volatility, ignores jumps/autocorrelation).

 Key Competitors and Alternatives
- Black-Scholes Model (1973 Baseline): Widely used for options pricing but empirically flawed. Studies (e.g., Frontiers in Applied Mathematics, 2024) show it underperforms in volatile markets. Alternatives like deep learning (arXiv, 2025) outperform BS in specific conditions, but lack real-time coherence analysis.
- Stochastic Volatility Models (e.g., Heston): Improve on BS by allowing variable volatility; common in hedge funds. However, they require heavy computation and still assume Brownian motion (LinkedIn analysis, 2021).
- Machine Learning Approaches: Neural networks (ScienceDirect, 2021-2025 papers) predict options better than BS under different regimes, but suffer from black-box issues and data overfitting. Examples: LSTM for pricing vs. BS (arXiv, 2025 comparative study shows ML superior in non-normal distributions).
- Other Alternatives: Bachelier model (normal distribution, better for low-vol assets; Palgrave Macmillan, 2025); Monte Carlo simulations; Binomial trees. Investopedia notes BS remains standard due to simplicity, but alternatives like these address fat tails.
- Emerging Quantum-Inspired Models: Sparse but growing (e.g., QuTiP in physics sims). No direct competitors to SEP's coherence-focused engine, which combines quantum flux handling with classical efficiency—positioning us ahead of pure ML or traditional models.

 SEP Dynamics' Differentiation
- Superior Edge: Quantum-inspired coherence quantification captures non-linear pattern evolution, outperforming BS (which ignores autocorrelation) and ML (which lacks physics grounding).
- Efficiency: Real-time GPU acceleration vs. compute-intensive Monte Carlo.
- IP Barrier: Proprietary kernels (QBSA/QFH) prevent easy replication.
- Market Positioning: Start as prop firm (low entry barriers), evolve to licensing—unlike pure software vendors (e.g., Bloomberg terminals) or hardware-dependent quantum firms.

SWOT: Strengths (proven tech, founder expertise); Weaknesses (early-stage); Opportunities (AI-fintech boom); Threats (regulatory changes, e.g., Volcker Rule remnants).
